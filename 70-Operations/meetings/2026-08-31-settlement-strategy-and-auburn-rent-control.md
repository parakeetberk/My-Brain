His read of plaintiffs' counsel is that they have real money on the table, are close to the goal line, and are looking for something they can package up and sell to their own side, which he treats as good news rather than a trap. Option one is to finish the CBRE analysis and hand the data over as the argument that the increases are reasonable, which keeps the number ours and spends disclosure to do it. Option two settles the case now with the rent schedule left open and set later by a jointly agreed appraiser, binding on both sides like an arbitration, which is faster but hands the number to a third party. Natalie's objection was that a Massachusetts appraiser will drift tenant friendly whatever the merits, and counsel answered it two ways: both sides have to agree on who the appraiser is, so no mobile home tenant advocate gets the job, and the settlement agreement can set a floor and a ceiling on the increases so the appraiser cannot come back at $400. Where it landed is counsel's own preference, stated late: push CBRE to deliver by end of week, and if CBRE's numbers give cover, take the appraiser route and let a third party close it permanently. That is a shift from the written position of three days earlier, when Jon had picked option one. Nobody hard-committed. Counsel speaks to Melissa at eleven the next morning, intends to follow her lead, and will circle back the same day. His closing read on why they are close: plaintiffs' counsel is working this for free, December and her year end are coming, and a firm with no appetite to negotiate would have said take it or leave it instead of proposing alternatives.
